Tomorrow, I’ll Be a Bad Kid

Abstract: I spent years learning how to behave. Perhaps it is finally time to learn how not to.

At 5:00 PM on December 21st, as the announcement “The exam is over” rang out, I thought to myself that my days in the exam-oriented education system were finally coming to an end. Yet, in the blink of an eye, I found myself wandering across the campus, all the way to the construction site near the quadrangle—no reason, no destination, like a dangling pointer drifting out of bounds, my body moving without me. A message from my roommate brought me back: “What’re you doing? The exam’s over, isn’t it?” Only then did I realise that I had already been free()’d. I posted a single line on QQ Space—“408’s so easy.”—and started back.

On the way back, Kasane Teto’s Science was playing through my headphones, but my mind was not moving with the music. I was not thinking about the exam itself. The questions had already begun to recede into the distance, and the answers with them. In their place was only an immense, shapeless emptiness.

What am I going to do tonight? Have dinner, then sleep? I woke up far too early this morning; I should probably rest.

And tomorrow? Should I start preparing my thesis? Or finally implement the zero-crossing algorithm for the 2M FSK radio?

The exam is over. There shouldn’t be anything left to suffer through.

But the Buddha said that life is a sea of suffering.

So what am I supposed to suffer from next? There will still be suffering in the future, won’t there? And yet, right now, there is none. Perhaps this is the happiest moment of my life: a brief interval in which there is absolutely nothing left to endure. Have even the Bodhisattvas ever encountered such a peculiar state?

Would it be all right if I simply died here, like this?

… So what am I supposed to do next?

Am I free now?

…

What, exactly, is freedom?

—Was I ever free at all?

The thought felt strangely out of place. Freedom seemed like a word that should not have appeared at that moment. I had just walked out of a system that had governed my life for more than ten years. In theory, this should have been the freest moment I had ever known. There were no more examinations waiting immediately ahead of me, no timetable to obey, no next checkpoint already marked on the calendar.

And yet, instead of feeling that I had finally gained something, I began to wonder whether freedom was something I had already lost somewhere along the way. It was not the first time I had felt that way.

Years ago, before I had learned how to be “adapted,” before I had even learned what counted as the given, it would never have occurred to me to ask myself questions like these.

I did not think of myself as free back then. Yet I would do all sorts of things that now seem strange: taking apart the family PC simply because I wanted to know what it looked like inside; nmapping against the teachers’ server during IT class because I was bored; building a crude EMP device and killing my father’s calculator after watching a science-fiction movie…

At the time, none of this felt mischievous, let alone rebellious. I was not trying to break rules, or prove anything to anyone. Things were simply there in front of me, and I wanted to know what was inside them.

Looking back, perhaps that was where many of the troubles that came later first began.

But then again— What, exactly, had I taken apart all those years ago?

At first, I thought they were only machines, programs, things I probably should not have touched. It was only much later that I began to realise that what I had been taking apart was not merely hardware.

Some things enter our lives very early as the given: adults are right; rules exist for a reason; hard work will be rewarded; debts must be repaid; obedient children are good children. They do not require explanation, much less proof. They simply exist, like air.

The first time I realised that some of these things might not be entirely true, I could not even tell whether what I felt was anger or confusion. There was only a vague uneasiness, a sense that the world did not quite fit the description I had been given.

I was still very young then, and had not yet learned to hide such feelings.

1. When the given no longer holds

“If I weren’t worried about tiring you out, I’d really like to make you run another couple of laps. How can you have no sense of justice at all?”

I remember those words remarkably clearly.

I was probably still in elementary school. We were out for a morning run when my grandfather said them to me. The reason was, in fact, quite simple: I had been defending my father, trying to explain that what had happened was not entirely his fault.

Those were years in which the global economy was going through its own turbulence. I only learned much later that such “fluctuations” were not merely abstractions in economics textbooks. They could descend, quite concretely, into the lives of particular people. A butterfly could flap its wings somewhere in America, and a storm could eventually arrive in our home.

Of course I knew what justice meant—or at least, until then, I had always thought I did.

Justice meant standing on the right side when two sides were in conflict.

The trouble is that once you define justice in that way, certain situations become very difficult to reason about.

My grandfather did not know all the details. I, however, watched at close range as assets worth nearly ten million turned, within a remarkably short time, into debt. There was no stock-market collapse, no exotic financial derivative, none of the elaborate fraud one sees in television dramas. There were only a few conversations, a few contracts, and an unremarkable signature on a piece of paper.

Now suppose we uphold the principle that “debts must be repaid” as the given. In that case, we are obliged to hand over money that my parents and the rest of the family had spent years earning—to people who we believed had quite plainly deceived us.

If those people stood on the side of the given, then where, exactly, did that leave us?

Did the mere fact that the final result was called debt automatically place us on the side of injustice?

Were all 13,268,809 people (8,746,845 as of 5 October 2026, if one is prepared to take the official figures at face value) in the country—nearly one percent of the population—therefore destined to belong to the unjust side?

That was perhaps the first time I realised that some of the sentences I had been taught since childhood, sentences I had never thought to question, did not seem to survive every encounter with reality.

Ordinarily they were like air: invisible, omnipresent, and in no need of justification. But the moment a sufficiently real situation appeared, the air suddenly became thin.

Curiously, I did not suffocate.

Instead, I began sorting through the axioms I had accumulated since childhood, picking them up one by one and asking whether they were actually true.

One of them was the Di Zi Gui—Standards for Being a Good Student and Child.

I must have been in first or second grade when our school made us memorise it. I can still picture the booklet: small, yellow, probably 32mo, with the characters printed neatly from left to right, line after line:

“Di Zi Gui, the teachings of the sages;
first filial piety and brotherly love,
then diligence and trustworthiness...”

The teacher had us recite it sentence by sentence. Whatever was printed there, we memorised.

Over the years, I have often wondered what exactly I felt as I read those lines.

I understood the words well enough. I recited them as I was told. And still, I wanted to cry.

It was a peculiar feeling, one I could not explain. The more I read, the stronger it grew. I no longer remember what I was thinking—perhaps I was not thinking anything at all. I only remember feeling strangely hurt and helpless, a sense of oppression, as though something invisible were pressing down on me.

The booklet itself was thin. Yet every line seemed to be saying:

You should be this way.

You must be this way.

I cannot remember whether I actually cried. I remember only that I never finished memorising the booklet. As for why, the simplest explanation is probably that my parents did not insist.

There had been another little yellow booklet in our home before that, almost the same size. It was not the Di Zi Gui, but a Buddhist prayer book my grandmother had brought back from Zhanshan Temple. It contained texts such as the Great Compassion Mantra and the Heart Sutra.

Naturally, I understood almost none of it. Its words were far more unfamiliar to me than anything in the Di Zi Gui.

And yet, strangely enough, I never resisted it.

I liked reciting it.

“Namo Ratna Trayaya…”

As I repeated those sounds, I would gradually grow quiet. Nobody was telling me what sort of person I ought to become. Nobody was urging me to behave in one way rather than another. I simply sat there, reciting along, while something inside me slowly settled.

Looking back many years later, I suspect that what disturbed me about the Di Zi Gui was not necessarily its individual teachings. It was the sensation of being hurried towards becoming a “correct person”—of having the given engraved into my consciousness before I had even been allowed to examine it, as though someone were pressing a steel seal into thoughts that had not yet finished taking shape.

May that was when I developed my fondness for taking things apart.

And traditional culture, precisely because so much of it had first been presented to me as unquestionable, naturally became one of my favourite targets.

“I don’t actually know whether this is right or wrong. But since we’re all here, why not take it apart and see? And if it falls apart—wouldn’t that be fun?”

This is, to this day, one of my favourite forms of dinner-table entertainment.

From trivial matters such as table manners to grander ones such as Confucian thought versus Confucianism as a religion, almost nothing escapes my instinct to say: let’s open it up and see what’s inside.

Most of these collisions between myself and the adults, of course, end with nobody persuading anybody. Eventually we retreat to that wonderfully diplomatic conclusion:

“We should preserve the essence and discard the dross.”

But I enjoy the process immensely.

One especially representative target has been the idea of etiquette, often beginning with the deceptively small question:

“Should I say thank you to my own family?”

Once, when I found myself losing such an argument, I finally threw every card I had onto the table:

“What is the point of that kind of etiquette? The relationship between my family and me is too close to be expressed through etiquette. I love you—but if I have to express that love through the form of etiquette, it feels as though I am contaminating the content itself. The Tao that can be told is not the eternal Tao; whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent. The moment I express it, its meaning changes. If formal etiquette cannot convey what I actually feel, then I would rather not use it at all. I’m fundamentally different from those primitive people in ancient times. I wasn’t trained to express myself through ritual. I SPEAK FROM THE HEART!”

That managed to leave my grandparents speechless.

Looking back on the argument later, I realised that the entire thing probably reduced to a dispute over where “excellent traditional virtues” ended and “feudal dross” began. We were not, in fact, disagreeing about anything nearly as fundamental as I had imagined.

Still, I loved it.

In arguments like these, I could feel my own existence with extraordinary clarity.

Perhaps this was what cogito, ergo sum was supposed to mean.

I could disagree with my elders. I could disagree with sages who had been marked as truth for two thousand years. More importantly, the people around me would actually allow me to disagree. They would sit there and argue back. They would enter the debate with me.

How many children in Shandong families, across all those centuries past and present, had been allowed to do that?

I was absurdly proud of myself.

Yet those arguments, those acts of dismantling, were never really about winning.

What I wanted was to confirm something much simpler:

Beyond all these things repeatedly presented to me as the given, was there still somewhere for me to stand?

Over the years, I dismantled many things.

But I never quite discovered where I belonged among the pieces.

Sometimes, when an argument ended and the dinner table finally fell quiet, a peculiar emptiness would return. It was as though I had successfully proved that I existed, but had not yet figured out what to do with that existence.

So, before the silence could settle, I would begin looking for the next thing to take apart.

And that was how I fell down the rabbit hole.

2. The Land of Nothingness

The rabbit hole did not lead only downward.

For a while, I practically lived on this habit of dismantling things. As long as there was still something left to take apart, something left to question, I had a way of proving to myself that I had not completely disappeared.

Eventually, though, it became exhausting.

I began to realise that, rather than continuing to dismantle everything around me, what I really wanted was to find a place—

a place where, for a little while, I would not have to dismantle anything at all.

No arguments. No proofs. No need to decide which side I was supposed to stand on.

I was in high school then.


High school did not arrive with any dramatic transformation. The world simply became, all at once, unbearably full.

Time was cut into neat blocks. Things had to be done in a prescribed order. There was rarely enough room left to take anything apart and look inside it. Almost all of my attention was consumed by a single rhythm:

Study. Study. Then the college entrance examination.

It was during those years that I began giving a name to a place that did not exist.

I called it the Land of Nothingness.

At the time, I had no clear idea what those words were supposed to mean.

I simply needed a name for somewhere that did not have to be arranged, measured, inspected, or made useful.

Could there be such a place, where it was only my family and me?


Later, I realised that I did not actually need to find the whole place at once. It was enough to steal back a small corner of it.

During lunch break, I disliked returning to the classroom. It was too noisy, and there were always people bent over their desks studying. Something about that atmosphere made me uncomfortable.

So I would often go upstairs and sit alone on a bench on the rooftop.

Most of the time I simply daydreamed. Sometimes I played with my phone. Sometimes I finished my homework.

There had once been an activity room there for the school robotics club, but the lights were almost always off, and hardly anyone ever came.

What use was a robotics club in a high school consumed by examinations, anyway?

That was exactly what made it such a good place for me. Nobody cared what I was doing there.

The school library was strange in a similar way. Its opening hours almost entirely overlapped with class time, making its existence seem, according to the logic of the timetable, faintly redundant.

For me, that redundancy became an opportunity.

For a while, I would skip the morning running exercises and hide in the library instead.

I studied calculus there.

Is calculus actually interesting?

Having just finished the postgraduate entrance examination, I must admit that I am no longer entirely convinced.

But compared with running exercises, calculus seemed remarkably gentle.

The symbols on the page did not ask me to stand straight, keep pace with anyone else, and shout in unison.

They simply remained there on the paper, quietly, whether I solved them quickly or slowly.


Gradually, I developed a habit of appearing in places where “nobody cared what I was doing,” at times when I was not really supposed to be there.

Often it did not even feel like a deliberate decision.

My body seemed to know the way before I did.

What I wanted was not better grades.

and not even “efficiency.”

I only wanted to know this:

after a day had been pulverised into schedules, exercises, bells, and obligations, would there still be one small fragment left that genuinely belonged to me?

Eventually I realised that those fragments were not isolated accidents.

They accumulated.

There were enough of them that, looking back, my high-school years did not seem to resemble what other people later described as the way high school was supposed to have been.


I did not begin to understand this until I went to college.

There, I began hearing my classmates talk about their own high-school years—sometimes online, sometimes in conversations that stretched late into the night in our dormitory.

What struck me was how casually they spoke, as though recounting something long since over, something that had nevertheless never quite left them.

They got up at five in the morning and returned to their dormitories at eleven, sometimes midnight.

Some ate only twice a day, with less than ten minutes for each meal.

During morning runs, they moved in tightly packed matrics, shoulder to shoulder, their footsteps falling in unison.

They memorised vocabulary while waiting in line for meals.

During evening self-study, they had to keep their heads down. Some schools even counted those who looked up. They called it the head-raising rate.

And during morning reading, some were made to stand with a book in one hand and the other arm raised high, a finger pointing straight at the ceiling, shouting the same lines over and over again.

Some memorised vocabulary while eating.

Some had learned to sleep standing up.

Some could scarcely remember how they had made it through those three years.

They spoke of classmates who had died. Of chronic stomach problems. Of high blood pressure, as though it were hardly worth mentioning. Some spoke of overdosing a way of making the feelings stop.

And when they talked about these things, they were not usually angry.

Sometimes they even laughed.

“Those years were really something.”

I listened and nodded from time to time, but I never quite knew how to join in.

Once, I said something like, “I don’t know. I usually got home pretty early and went to bed at around ten.”

They stared at me as though I had said something shocking.

I did not know how to respond to their astonishment.

Their stories were not entirely foreign to the world I had inhabited. The same structures, the same expectations, the same pressure had existed around me.

And yet somehow, those particular scenes had never quite become my own.

Instead, I remembered the rooftop bench during lunch break, and the long stretches of time I spent there doing nothing.

I remembered hiding in the library and secretly teaching myself calculus.

I remembered all those “inappropriate times” spent in places where “nobody cared what I was doing.”

And only then did I begin to understand:

it was not that I had lived without pressure.

I had simply learned, very early, to route myself around it.


Not to escape it entirely—that would have been impossible. The pressure was still there.

But I had found small openings in it, passages through which some part of me could reach somewhere else.

The Land of Nothingness had never really been empty. It was simply a place that asked nothing of me.

And yet, even there, something was missing.

I did not want to disappear there alone.

I wanted my family there with me.


Smartphones were forbidden in high school.

—At least, that was what the rules said. It did not concern me very much.


The school had issued each of us a tablet for studying. It was a carefully enclosed little system: what you were allowed to do, what you were allowed to install, where you were allowed to go—almost everything had already been decided in advance.

For most students, it was exactly what it was intended to be, and probably would remain so forever:

a study tool.

To me, however, it looked more like a tightly locked door.


What happened next was probably inevitable.

At first, I did not intend to “tear the door down.” That sounded far too dramatic.

I merely noticed how thoroughly everything had been restricted—so thoroughly that even the most elementary forms of connectivity seemed to have been treated as unnecessary.

And at that particular point in my life, connectivity was precisely what I needed most.


So I began emailing my family.

Sometimes the messages were only a few lines long: a complaint about that day’s schedule, a trivial observation, a question about who would pick me up that evening.

My parents always seemed to reply quickly.

Sometimes almost suspiciously quickly.


Those messages solved no practical problem. They changed no timetable, cancelled no class, and removed no rule.

But they reassured me of something far more important:

the doors to the outside world had not been completely sealed.


Gradually, though, another realisation followed.

If I wanted that connection to remain, then the tablet could not remain entirely as it had been permitted to be.


So—

let us take the door apart.

Apparently, I had returned to my old habits.


Software, bootloaders, locked-down operating systems—they turned out not to be so different from the traditions and assumptions I had spent years dismantling in my own head.

Sometimes, one does not even need anything deserving the grand title of a “hacking technique.”

Sometimes the easiest solution is simply to reject the premise on which the whole thing was built, take it apart at the foundation, and rebuild it according to a different set of assumptions.

“I broke the tablet against the blackboard,” I told my parents. “I think I need to replace the hard drive.”

That was more or less how it began.

Later, I found an EDL flashing package that made the hardware modification unnecessary. But that came afterwards. The beginning had been far more primitive, and much more in character:

if the door would not open, I would make an opening.


And after I did, nothing particularly dramatic happened.

The tablet still lay quietly in my backpack, now with a few pieces of disguise software installed, looking almost exactly as harmless as before.

I went to class as usual.

I did my homework as usual—depending, as always, on my mood.

At lunch, I continued disappearing into the places where nobody cared what I was doing.

On weekends, I tinkered with the device I had finally tamed.

The only real difference was that home was easier to reach.

Sometimes a message arrived while I was standing around a corner in the hallway.

Sometimes during the short break before evening self-study.

Sometimes even just after I had woken from a nap in Chinese class.

Only a few lines of text.

Yet every time, they reminded me of the same thing:

I had not been completely cut off.


Of course, something like this could not remain invisible forever.

I had published a technical note about the tablet on my blog, and before long the method began circulating around the school. Eventually, inevitably, someone noticed.

The day I was called into the teacher’s office, there had been no particular warning.

My grades had not suddenly collapsed.

I had not caused trouble in class.

I hadn’t even had the opportunity to get our stories straight with my classmates.

Someone had simply noticed that a device which was supposed to be “operating according to regulations” was doing rather too many things that it was apparently not supposed to do.

Sitting in that familiar office, I understood something with unusual clarity:

the system and I were describing the same act in completely different languages.

To me, it was connection.

It was need.

To them, it was violation.

From their perspective, a learning device had been “misused.”

From mine, I had simply found a way to remain in contact with my family.

Even what followed—a talk with my parents, then my temporary suspension from evening self-study—failed to frighten me.

If anything, I felt relieved.

“Why don’t you skip evening self-study for the rest of the week,” the teacher said, “and go home to reflect on what you’ve done?”

“Okay.”

I answered almost immediately.

No excuses. No bargaining.

Before sunset, I collected my exit pass, bought myself a cup of milk tea, and walked home briskly along the shaded path beside the ditch.

I remember the light that evening more clearly than I remember the punishment.

When Mum came home from my school, she looked at me, smiled, and teased:

“Well, the boy has finally grown up. This is the first time I’ve ever been called in as a parent.”

Then she proceeded to “brag” about how casually she had chatted with the teacher, recounting the whole encounter with the satisfaction of an accomplished social engineer.

She did not ask me why I had done it.

She did not scold me for always finding new ways to cause trouble.

She did not demand a promise that it would never happen again.

And she did not say the sentence I had heard in so many other forms throughout my life:

“Why can everyone else do it? Why are you the only one who has to be different?”

Instead, the question underneath everything seemed much simpler.

Are you still here?

Are you all right?

That was enough.


And suddenly, I understood something about the Land of Nothingness.

Not a place outside the rules.

Just a place where someone stayed beside me when the rules grew too dense to breathe.

A place where I did not need to translate myself back into a version that had been approved in advance.

Where I could remain myself—

and still remain connected.

Me,

and my family,

with no need to return to my “permitted self.”

The Land of Nothingness I had spent so long imagining—

perhaps I had never truly left it.

But knowing this did not make everything else disappear.

Some things remain inside the body long after the circumstances that produced them are gone.


One night, shortly before my postgraduate entrance examination, I dreamt I was back at military training.

I remember almost nothing of what happened. There was no sequence to follow, no beginning or end. Only a feeling I recognised at once.

The air was stifling.

There were too many people.

And I should not have been there.


I woke with my heart pounding. For a moment, I could not even say what had frightened me.

But the feeling stayed with me, long after the dream itself had faded.

Somewhere in that crowd, I had felt it again:

the fear of being asked to surrender the fact

that I was an individual.

3. We Are Human

In the dream, the first thing I saw,

was the roof.

The military-training base had a great curved roof supported by wooden beams, almost like an enormous greenhouse standing in the middle of a field. Steel rods and cables crossed overhead, and rows of metal chandeliers hung beneath them at regular intervals.

I stood below and looked up.

The ceiling was perhaps three stories high, yet it never felt high enough.

It seemed to be descending slowly towards me.

The air was heavy. I remember breathing carefully.

Perhaps I was looking upward. Perhaps I was not.

What I remember more clearly is the feeling that, while standing there, every step I was supposed to take and every place I was supposed to occupy had already been decided for me.

It was not exactly painful.

It was not even exhaustion.

It was more like standing inside a world in which there was no longer any need for me to make decisions.


Near the entrance was a small shelter with an overhanging roof.

When we lined up before meals, it was the only patch of shade nearby.

We stood beneath the sun.

They stood beneath the eaves.

Before we were allowed to eat, we had to shout slogans.

Again and again.

Whoever shouted the best would eat first.

The definition of “best” was remarkably simple.

Clarity? Precision? No, it was volume.

So I tried to shout louder.

My throat tightened.

No sound came out.

I did not know whom I was competing against.

The same familiar sentences seemed to play endlessly, like recordings through a loudspeaker:

“Where is your fighting spirit?”

“What have you been training for?”

“Why can everyone else do it, but you can’t?”

I stood there listening.

I did not know how to answer.

Maybe there was no answer at all.

Whatever I said would already have been wrong.


Eventually, we were allowed inside to eat.

The building was enormous.

The kitchen stood immediately to the right of the entrance. On its door was a sign. It did not say No Unauthorised Entry.

It said:

Idlers, Keep Out.

At the time, I did not really understand what the phrase meant.

I only remember thinking that it seemed to have nothing to do with me—

and somehow seemed to be addressed directly to me.

The serving windows looked much like those in our school cafeteria.

Beyond them were several rows of long tables, arranged so that an entire collective could sit together As One.

Even there, everyone’s place seemed to have been decided beforehand.

Once, someone was pushed out of position by the crowd and was immediately stopped and reprimanded.

Then we stood beside the tables.

And waited.

We did not know whom we were waiting for.

We did not know what we were waiting for.

Until someone shouted:

“Sit!”

Everyone sat down together.

Caps were placed to one side in unison.

Postures were corrected.

Then we waited again.

“Eat!”

Only then did we begin.

Everyone sat within the small rectangle of space allotted to them.

Nobody spoke.

Nobody looked up.

Sometimes our own teachers—the ones who actually taught us something—brought us porridge.

The porridge was extremely thin. There were barely any grains of rice in the bucket.

I remember that detail because I later took my turn on meal duty.

There was a slogan printed on the bucket.

I no longer remember its exact wording.

I only remember that it did not sound like a gentle reminder—nothing like Remember how hard food is to come by.

It sounded more like an accusation.

There were slogans elsewhere too.

Some were eventually removed.

But the one on the rubbish bin remained:

TURNING TREASURE INTO WASTE （变宝为废）

The paint had begun to wear away.

A few strokes were missing from the characters.

But I could always read them.

The rubbish bin remained, of course, a rubbish bin.

It was only those words that made me pause in front of it every time.


For a long time, I believed that feelings like these belonged to childhood.

An unnamed fear.

Something that would gradually fade as I grew older.

After all, I had learned to adapt.

I had learned when to remain silent.

When to cooperate.

When to put an uncomfortable feeling aside and return to it later.

At some point, I even convinced myself that I had placed enough distance between myself and that world.

Then, many years later, during university military training, I found myself standing under the same kind of sun again.

And I realised that some things do not simply disappear with age.

They change shape.

Then wait for you to recognise them.


That day, we were queuing for a COVID-19 nucleic-acid test.

The line was long, and time seemed barely to move.

A few students sat down while waiting.

Some crossed their legs.

Others leaned quietly against the railing.

Nothing unusual was happening.

Nobody was making noise.

Nobody was fooling around.

Then someone noticed them.

What followed happened quickly.

The person walked over.

They did not call anyone by name.

They did not ask what had happened.

They simply spoke in that familiar tone:

“With an attitude like this, do you have any sense of collective discipline at all?”

And then:

“Why can everyone else stand properly without crossing their legs? Why are you the only ones who have to be different?”

Then we were made to stand there together for half an hour.

The sun was almost directly overhead.

Our shadows had shrunk beneath our feet.

There was nowhere to hide.

I stood there and felt the same suffocating sensation return.

It was not exhaustion.

It was not the heat.

It was the knowledge that this was not really a correction.

It was a demonstration.

Someone had to be made into an example so that everyone else would understand.

I desperately wanted to say something.

I wanted to say:

Sitting down is not the same thing as laziness.

I wanted to say:

Punishment should not expand into collective punishment.

I even wanted to ask:

By what authority do you decide what posture a young person must adopt in order to count as properly behaved?

The sentences were already arranged inside my head.

Logical—

though the situation itself seemed to require no logic.

Controlled—

though no one appeared interested in what anyone felt.

I had even anticipated the likely rebuttals and prepared replies to them.


But I said nothing.

I stood where I had been told to stand and swallowed the sentences one by one.

Because I knew exactly what would happen if I spoke.

Nothing would change.

I would simply become visible.

I would become someone to watch.

Someone who required special attention.

And so I remained silent.


Around noon, I slipped into the bathroom and called my mother.

I told her that I was “probably going to have a stomachache” that afternoon.

She understood immediately.


Had I simply refused to return to the playground, I would not have had to stand there for the rest of the afternoon.

Perhaps I would have received another reprimand.

Perhaps that would have made a rather fine medal after all.


But I backed down.


Not because anyone forced me to.

That was precisely what disturbed me.

I had already become remarkably good at calculating the consequences.

I knew where resistance was safe.

I knew where it would become inconvenient.

I knew when silence was the cheapest option.

No one needed to order me to retreat anymore.


I could do it myself…


Even now, I sometimes picture myself standing beneath that sun.

It took me years to understand why that memory stayed with me.

I had learned to swallow my own words.

I had learned to keep my body exactly where it had been placed.


And if time could truly run backward—

even if nothing I said could have changed the outcome,

even if all I could have done was take one small step forward,

I do not think I would cooperate with that silence quite so easily again.


Because I have become human.

4. Science!

Realising all of this did not solve anything in the real world. The questions were still there. Time was still moving. The examination would not be postponed simply because I happened to be uncertain about what any of it meant. There was only one thing left to do: keep going. The postgraduate entrance examination was only a few months away. Around that time, I began listening to one song over and over again: Kasane Teto’s Science. There is a line in it:

存在証明　見つけたい。
I want to find proof that I exist.

For those few months, my life became unusually monotonous. I would wake up, sit down, open a book, and turn through page after page of problems. Some I could solve. Some I could not. The ones I could not solve, I marked. I no longer felt any particular urgency to understand them immediately. Perhaps the most noticeable change was that I had stopped waiting for some grand moment of enlightenment. I simply did whatever I could manage that day and left the remainder to the next.

Often, I was not even sure whether what I was doing counted as “studying.” I did not want to get out of bed in the morning. Once I did, I would make a cup of coffee, sit at my desk for a while, and only then decide whether I felt like opening a book. Sometimes the entire morning disappeared that way. It would be lunchtime before I suddenly noticed: Apparently I haven’t done anything today.

At moments like that, a familiar anxiety would seep back in. Not panic. Nothing dramatic. Just a slow, persistent uneasiness. I knew that I was supposed to work harder. At the same time, I did not want “working harder” to become yet another mechanism for draining myself dry.

Around me, people were becoming increasingly tense.

How many rounds of mathematics review had they finished?

How many sets of English papers?

How many times had they gone through the material for their major subjects?

How many politics questions had they completed?

These numbers appeared constantly in conversations, like some universally accepted progress bar. I rarely joined in. None of those numbers could tell me whether I would still be capable of sitting down and studying tomorrow.

Several times, when I could no longer bear sitting in my room, I packed a few books and my laptop and travelled several kilometres to the north-campus library. I could concentrate somewhat better there. My efficiency was still nothing impressive. My favourite seat was No. 168 on the fifth floor. It had a power outlet and, more importantly, a window. From the top floor, I could look outside and see trees, sky, and a little distance.

Sometimes it reminded me of the days before the college entrance examination. Back then, too, I sat in the last row beside a window. Whenever class became unbearable, I would look outside towards the road that led home.

The south-campus library rarely offered anything like that. Most of its seats were arranged like a classroom: rows upon rows of tables, few windows, nothing much to look at, every seat occupied by someone bent over a book.

An examination factory.

The north-campus library usually did not become like that until four or five in the afternoon. Before then, I could still find a little space around myself. After four or five, more people would begin arriving. The sun would be getting ready to set. And for me, that usually meant the day was over. I would put my laptop back into my bag, sling it over my shoulder, and walk out against the stream of people coming in—the most boring kind of “retrograde.”

A few times, someone left a note beside me: Please type more quietly. The keyboard is really loud. After that, I generally made sure to leave before the library became crowded. I still do not know how intolerably loud a laptop membrane keyboard can really be. But I was certain of one thing: I never wanted to force myself into such a state of tension that the tapping of someone else’s keys became unbearable.

Sometimes I went to Starbucks instead. I would order a slightly extravagant flat white, find a corner, open my laptop, and study there. Starbucks played music, so nobody complained about my keyboard. Oddly enough, it often felt quieter than the silent library. May because I could no longer hear everyone else screaming inside their heads.

On other days, I did not want to think about anything at all. I would walk alone to Puzhao Temple. Not for searching for enlightenment, not even particularly searching for peace. Sometimes I brought a book and read for a while. More often, I simply sat there and stared at the bamboo. And somehow, spending a day that way often felt more worthwhile than spending a day studying at school. At least, for those few hours, I had not been entirely consumed by the question of what I was supposed to be doing.

The days continued to pass. I did not become noticeably more disciplined. But I did not stop, either.

Some material I reviewed again and again.

Other material I abandoned, deliberately or otherwise.

Gradually, I became almost numb to the concept of “time.” The numbers on the calendar kept changing. I rarely looked up. Until one day, quite suddenly, I realised that there was not much time left. The dramatic feeling people describe as the countdown has begun never arrived. Instead, I found myself asking increasingly practical questions.

Is this chapter still worth studying now?

Will finishing this problem set actually help me?

If I feel terrible today, should I force myself to continue—or should I simply stop?

I stopped trying to make each day “complete.” Some things—KMP, red-black trees—I simply skipped. Some standard methods clearly did not suit me. So I abandoned them. None of these decisions made me feel particularly good. But at least they made me resent the person sitting at my desk a little less.

Eventually, even staying at school became difficult.

The environment itself had begun to fill me with a pessimism I couldn’t name.

One evening, I was suddenly seized by a very specific desire:

I want to go home.

There was surprisingly little deliberation. I called home. Then I bought a train ticket for the following morning. The next day, I boarded the train with almost nothing: a backpack, a few books, and my laptop.

The carriage was quiet.

I sat beside the window and did nothing.

Going home did not feel nearly as dramatic as I had imagined.

There was no—

Finally, I’m free.

No—

Now I can pull myself together.

Only the ordinary feeling of returning to somewhere familiar.

At home, I continued studying. There was no fixed schedule. Sometimes I worked in the morning. Sometimes in the afternoon. Sometimes I sat at my desk and managed only a few pages.

But I stopped asking myself so often:

Is this acceptable?

My days gradually became more regular. There was no more “25:00, at dorm” routine. Nobody recorded how many hours I had studied. Nobody reminded me that I ought to be working harder.

Several times, I stopped halfway through simply because I felt I had done enough for the day. It was hardly a rigorous standard. Yet it meant that studying no longer had to be one continuous act of coercion.

Strangely enough, despite this apparent “indulgence,” I became much more efficient than I had been at school.

Almost without noticing, I finished the mathematics material I had planned to cover and worked through data structures. Then I finally began studying politics—the subject I had spent the previous three months repeatedly attempting, and repeatedly failing, to start.

About three days before the examination, I returned to school. The campus looked almost unchanged. The people looked almost unchanged. But the atmosphere had tightened considerably.

During those final days, I devoted most of my energy to politics. From the beginning, I had never intended to “study it systematically.” I knew I could not. And I had no desire to force myself to pretend otherwise. My plan was brutally simple: memorise the answer templates my teacher had given us, line by line, without worrying too much about whether I “truly understood” them. If something similar appeared on the examination, I only needed to have something to write.

Politics is not an easy subject. It may not look as intellectually demanding as mathematics or as technically detailed as computer science. People often dismiss it as: “Just memorise it.” But memorisation is hardly simple.

I spent roughly one day memorising the basic principles of Marxism, which, comparatively speaking, was the easy part. What other people might spend a lifetime debating, a roomful of students can apparently memorise in an afternoon. Of course, the version we were expected to learn was considerably simpler. To put it rather unfairly: it sometimes seemed to begin with proposition A, then remind you that not-A also contained a reasonable side, then instruct you to view the issue comprehensively and “dialectically,” after which almost anything could be derived. Not so different, really, from all those “Youth should be such-and-such” slogans from military training.

Because pigs can fly => I am king.

Ex falso quodlibet.

One evening, a roommate asked me: “But if matter exists independently of consciousness, then—” I immediately interrupted him. “Don’t think about it too deeply. You won’t be able to memorise it anymore. lol.”

Unfortunately, the rest of the material was not nearly so cooperative. A sentence such as: “Respond to changes in the development environment, grasp the initiative in development, enhance core competitiveness…” contained no obvious proposition A, no intuitive chain of reasoning, nothing for my mind to hold onto. It was simply a sequence of phrases. Sometimes almost a sequence of characters.

I spent more than an hour trying to memorise fewer than two hundred characters.

I failed.

I tried again.

Failed.

Tried once more.

Failed again.

Eventually I surrendered and went back to the much more comprehensible outline of modern Chinese history.

Even on the night before the examination, I still could not reliably remember whether “ideological and political work” was supposed to be a “fundamental guarantee,” a “lifeline,” a “distinctive political advantage,” or a “landmark measure.”

A few stubborn propositions remained unmemorised. Several problem-solving techniques remained unlearned. But that was fine. This was not what I was good at. I decided to sleep at ten that night and wake at four the next morning to continue. At around nine, I watched an online tutorial on how to extract usable answers from the source material when you had no idea what else to write. More than 46,000 people were watching. Apparently, many of us were in more or less the same situation. I was fairly certain, however, that I would go to sleep earlier than most of them.

At around four in the morning, Alice woke me as planned. It was still completely dark. The last time I remembered waking at such an hour with such a feeling had been in high school.

My clock read:

28:15.

I was still using a thirty-hour clock at the time. It provided a peculiar form of psychological comfort: one o’clock in the morning did not have to be 1:00 tomorrow.

It could simply be 25:00 today.

I climbed out of bed as quietly as I could. My roommate was still asleep. There were only two of us left in the dormitory by then. Most of the other students taking the postgraduate entrance examination had already moved into hotels. I switched on the light. The desk looked exactly as it had the night before: two or three piles of study materials scattered across it, the top page bearing some phrase like: “Changes Unseen in a Century Are Acceleratingly Evoluting…” I still had two major questions left to memorise. So I sat down and continued.

Eventually the clock reached 29:59.

Then it became 06:00.

That was the end.

I put on my clothes and stepped outside. The sun had not risen. There was not even refracted light on the horizon to pretend that sunrise had begun. I had no idea whether the cafeteria would be open. I simply kept walking. My body felt slightly detached from me. My mental ring buffer was full, and every new line was overwriting an old one. Fortunately, a few cafeteria stalls were already open. I bought a sandwich and some porridge—enough to fill my stomach.

Then I put on my almost-empty backpack and headed outside. Quite a few people were waiting for rides by the road. Were they all going to the examination? I had no idea. I got into the taxi I had booked and set off. Almost casually, I posted on QQ Space: Going to the Exam~. As though the person going had very little to do with me. 神のまにまに was playing through my headphones. And somewhere along the way, I suddenly realised: there was nothing left to prepare.

When I reached the main campus, I still had plenty of time. I scanned my student card and walked into the library. The machine greeted me: “You are the 1st reader of the day.” For a moment, I could hardly believe there had finally come a day when I was working this hard. The truth, of course, was less impressive. The library had not actually opened yet. I was kicked out almost immediately, still holding the politics notes I had failed to finish memorising. And I knew that was it.

そうさ、神のまにまに、仰せのままに……

I walked into the examination room without much emotion. The supposedly “life-changing moment” arrived. The doki-doki feeling so many classmates had described did not.

I found my seat. Sat down. Placed my pen and admission ticket where they belonged. And waited.

When the first examination began, time became strangely neutral. It did not race past. It did not slow down enough to frighten me. I turned through the pages. Some questions yielded immediately. Others needed to be read several times.

At some point, I noticed something almost surprising: the questions were not entirely made of the things I had feared. They were not necessarily my strongest areas. But neither were they completely alien. Perhaps that was luck. I only remember feeling that, for once, the world did not seem particularly interested in making things difficult for me.

For the politics questions, I wrote down whatever I could remember. When memory failed, I became something like a language model: predict the next token, continue with whatever seemed statistically plausible, and try not to break coherence. I think that was the true essence of politics after all.

When the paper ended, I performed a quick estimate. About twenty-five points from multiple choice. Perhaps another twenty-five from the written section.

Fifty.

Good enough.

At noon, I bought a can of Monster Energy. The stereotype of the computer-science student surviving on Monster was already frightening enough— although I was, regrettably, not wearing my serafuku like a femboy programmer. I drank it anyway. I did not particularly want to fall asleep that afternoon.

The following examinations passed in much the same way: English. Mathematics. 408. Each produced roughly the same internal verdict:

Seems about right.

Probably okay.

English felt very easy.

Mathematics also felt easy, although I made far too many mistakes.

Never mind.

For the computer-science examination, roughly a third of the seats around me were empty.

The questions seemed easy too.

There were still plenty I did not know.

Never mind.

A hundred points would probably be enough.

Some questions happened to overlap with things I had encountered outside the examination system. One of the larger computer-architecture problems, for example, did not register in my mind as an “exam question” at all. I simply recognised it as the same kind of problem I had run into while researching RISC-V. There was no excitement in that realisation. Not even relief. I simply kept writing. Perhaps with another paper, another year, another set of topics, everything would have been different. I do not know. But on that particular day, in that particular room, the questions happened to allow me to answer them in a language I already knew.

And then, at five o’clock in the afternoon on December 21st, the loudspeaker announced:

“The exam is over.”

I left the room feeling almost nothing.

I posted one sentence on QQ Space:

408’s so easy.

My classmates were already complaining about how strange that year’s questions had been. I did not particularly care. I wandered around campus without much sense of direction, eventually beginning the journey back towards the south campus. On the bus, Teto’s Science was playing in my headphones again. My mind, however, was still running somewhere else. Thought after thought passed through it, apparently analysing something, though I no longer knew what. Only when the bus reached my stop did I finally notice where I was.

The doors opened.

People began stepping off.

So I stood up and followed them.

Outside, the wind on the south campus was cold.

It was already dark.

The music continued in my headphones,

but I could no longer quite make out the words.

This whole postgraduate entrance examination thing—

for now,

was over.

fin.

A few days after the examination,

time suddenly seemed to loosen.

The calendar slipped quietly into late December.

The city, however, looked much the same.


That day was Christmas.

I walked through the streets.

There were almost no Christmas trees.

None of the familiar music seemed to be playing.

The shopping malls looked much as they always did. People hurried past as usual, as though the holiday had simply failed to happen.

We had bought a Christmas tree for our home.

But somehow, it felt as though Christmas had been confined there with us.

Around that time, an official account published an article titled:

“Remember! December 24 Is the Day of Victory in the Battle of Chosin Reservoir.”

I did not actually read it myself. I only heard about it afterwards, and what I heard was rather disappointing.

The reactions online were scattered.

Some repeated that we must “never forget national humiliation.”

Some seemed unsure what the Battle of Chosin Reservoir even was.

Others pointed out that its supposed “day of victory” was not, in fact, December 24.


The idea that one “should not celebrate Western holidays” has circulated for many years.

I have always found it strangely difficult to explain why it makes me uncomfortable.

I am not Christian.

Christmas has never been, for me, some sacred tradition that must be defended.

It is much simpler than that.

It is a permission to relax.

An excuse.

An opportunity.

A tree.

A few strings of lights.

A family sitting together over dinner.

Children being allowed, for one evening, to imagine what gifts they might like to receive.

There is no mission attached to it.

It does not have to mean anything.

And perhaps that is precisely what I like about it.


Walking through the streets that evening, realising that even this small and largely meaningless thing seemed to have been quietly removed, I suddenly understood that what bothered me was not the loss of a holiday.


It was the feeling that even a day had to be told what it meant.

Some days must be remembered.

Some days must be forgotten.

Some days must be mourned.

Some days must prove something.

And some days, apparently, are not allowed simply to be light.


Yet I realised that I was still doing the same thing I had been doing for years:

quietly reserving a little space for myself outside meanings that had already been arranged in advance.

As a child, it meant dismantling the ideas that had been stamped into me.

In high school, it meant cracking open a locked tablet.

At university, it meant walking out of the library against the flow of people coming in.

And now, perhaps, it means nothing more dramatic than refusing to replace every day that could have been light with something heavier.


Tomorrow, I will probably continue studying.

I will continue living.

I will continue operating inside this system.

There is no Exodus waiting for me.

But perhaps there are still a few places where I no longer have to be quite so obedient.

I do not have to rush to align myself.

I do not have to compete to declare the correct opinion.

I do not have to pretend that I feel nothing.

And I do not have to turn every part of myself into my permitted self.

If that makes me a “bad kid,”

then I think I can live with that.

Tomorrow,

I am going to be a bad kid.

Sophiel
